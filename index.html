In short

A jammer does not have to drown a whole 5G uplink slot. Jamming a band of a few resource blocks is enough: it spoils the pilots the receiver learns the channel from and the data symbols in that band, and a standard receiver, which trusts every cell, then fails to decode. We let the receiver find, cell by cell, where a jammer transmitted, using only what it measures in the slot, and decode the same slot again with that map: jammed pilots are left out of the channel estimate and the bits of jammed data cells count less. All results come from simulations of a single-antenna 5G NR uplink.

Terms used on this page
PRB, OFDM symbol, cell
A slot is a grid: 24 physical resource blocks (PRBs, 12 subcarriers each) by 14 OFDM symbols. One PRB on one symbol is a cell.
Pilots (DMRS)
Known reference symbols, here on symbol 2, from which the receiver estimates the channel.
Jammer power (JSR)
The jammer's power per jammed cell relative to the user's signal, in dB: 0 dB means equal, −15 dB means about 30 times weaker.
SNR
The user's signal power relative to the receiver noise, in dB.
Blocks lost (BLER)
The share of transport blocks that fail their CRC check after decoding.
False alarm
A clean cell (or slot) that the detector flags anyway. Thresholds are set so that 1% of clean validation cells are flagged.
EVM
How far the equalized data symbols lie from the ideal QPSK points, relative to the expected noise.

How it works, step by step

The slot from the top of the page, followed through the receiver in five stages. The receiver first does what any receiver does: it estimates the channel from the pilots and equalizes the data. From the slot and those equalized symbols it measures three clues per cell, a small U-Net turns them into a map of the jammed cells, and the receiver decodes the same buffered samples again with that map.     Abstract

    A jammer can attack only part of a 5G NR uplink slot: the pilots (DMRS), the data symbols, or a band of physical resource blocks (PRBs). We estimate, for every PRB and OFDM symbol, whether a jammer transmitted there. Three quantities of the received slot feed a small U-Net: the received power P, a pilot residual R (the part of the least-squares pilot estimates outside a delay-limited channel subspace, whose distribution in noise we derive for an ideal channel model), and the decision-directed error vector magnitude E. A map-aware receiver then decodes the same buffered slot again, giving flagged pilots and the bits of flagged data cells less weight. In simulations of a single-antenna PUSCH link, the map reaches a cell F1 of 0.952 at a 1% validation false-alarm target. On TDL-C at 15 dB SNR, decoding with it lowers the block error rate under pilot, data and partial-band jamming at +9 dB JSR from 33.5–87.8% to at most 0.06%.

    Limitations

    • Everything is simulated: one receive antenna, one MCS, perfect synchronization, and, in the nominal case, a noise-like jammer aligned with the slot grid.
    • Detection lags block errors at low SNR: the smallest margin is 8.6 dB at 15 dB SNR but −6.1 dB at 5 dB SNR (TDL-C).
    • The detector misses part of a weak jammer: at −15 dB JSR the U-Net finds 57% of the jammed pilot cells (detection results).
    • A jammer that hops between PRBs from symbol to symbol needs a detector trained on such jammers; the nominally trained map leaves 21% BLER on hopping data jamming at +9 dB (see a hopping slot).
    • If every pilot of the allocation is jammed, no clean pilot remains. Data-aided passes (decoded data cells used as extra pilots) lower the BLER with the U-Net map at +9 dB JSR and 15 dB SNR from 39% to 24%, but not to zero, and with the noise power estimated from the jammed pilot comb even the true map does not beat the standard receiver (see that slot).
    • The detector also flags legitimate interference such as a neighbouring UE; telling intent apart needs other information.
